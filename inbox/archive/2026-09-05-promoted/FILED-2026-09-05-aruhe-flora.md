## Source (verbatim)

Aruhe is one big garden that never learned to stop. From the first dark stone above the shingle to the last clearing over the lake, everything that grows is a real plant doing a job it already knew how to do — just harder, larger, and slightly past sense. Here is what actually lives there.

**At the water’s edge**

The landing is shingle, not soil. The first plant you meet is a dark moss that stains the tideline. Survivors treat that line as the start of the island’s claim. Past it the terrace risers start, and the garden takes over.

**The old terraces**

Somebody cut these steps once. Roots pulled them apart. What grows there now is the same crop gone feral.

Yam vines thick as a wrist run every gap. Ordinary citrus hangs fist-sized and too many, sweet toward rot. Eat either and a body mends a little. The air on the slope tastes of fruit all afternoon.

Two terrace fruits do more than that. **Redheart berries** grow as big as a child’s fist, so dark-red they look black until you break them. The juice stains. The flesh inside is wet and the same color as a fresh cut. A wounded animal that finds one visibly comes back from it. They hang in dozens along the stone.

**Lion citrus** is the other abundant one: rind the color of a lion’s coat, peel coming off in one warm coil, flesh sweet and sharp. The oils in the skin wake you up and hold you together for a while.

**Giant’s guava** starts here and follows the tree line inland. Skull-sized, pink-gold, so ripe it gives under a thumb. The smell is fruit with something animal under it. Eat one and you are as strong as the big things that use these trails.

Between the levels, old irrigation ditches stand green with algae and drowned blossom. Some stretches are easier to wade than to walk the broken stone.

Huge flowers open on the same terraces. They are not named. Caterpillars live in them. Predators walk around any bloom that ticks. One flower holding several of those caterpillars is a hot bloom — leave it.

The rest of the terrace vines look like rope. Most of them are. A few are **vine-lash**: shoulder-thick, leafless, hanging like every other vine until something walks under. They feed through the roots. They are thickest where stone gives an anchor and canopy gives height.

Young **snakewood** hunts here too — the same family, not yet sixty feet of it. The adults stay inland so they do not crowd the terrace vines out.

**The mute jungle**

Above the last riser the canopy closes. Trunks too thick. Leaves the size of shields. Aerial roots hanging in curtains. Sight dies in a few paces. This is ordinary rainforest pushed until size and silence are the whole weather.

Game trails are the only reliable lines, packed open by animals. **Strangler figs** sit where those trails pinch between stone. Broad-trunked, ancient, aerial roots in curtains. Real strangler figs already kill their host tree. These kept going. One root finds an ankle. The trunk is hollow. Bones show in the bark where the wood grew over old kills. They grow singly. One is enough.

**Snakewood** owns the sealed canopy. It is not one vine. It is a clonal colony from one root crown: dozens of stems grafted until water and sugar pass between them. At rest it is just another mass of dull green-brown lianas, thick as an arm where several twist together. When it strikes, half a dozen stems open like fingers, clap shut, and the body on the ground goes up. There is no head. The “jaws” are whichever stems are braided that moment. Inside those jaws, when they open, sit tiny pale flowers that smell intensely sweet. Then the jaw closes and the flowers vanish. It digests outside itself — pale sap over whatever it has reeled into the trees. Trails dodge certain branches. Things that fall off a carcass stay on the ground. Everything edible went up.

**Stonepear** is common under this mute roof: grey-green, hard as pumice, heavy enough to thud when you set it down. Bite through the grit and the inside is sweet, cold, and briefly mineral. Animals eat one before they fight.

**Ghost plum** is less common. Pale blue-white like milk glass, bloom so thick it catches light the way fog does. In dim light the fruit loses its outline. Eat one and you drop out of sight for a while, until you strike or speak a spell.

**Stillbloom** starts where this green first goes black at the leaf edge — game trails and sunbreaks, not the open valleys. Most of the year it is a dull four-foot stalk, waxy, almost boring. Rings of pale hollow bracts sit on the upper third, each ending in a dark needle a few inches long. Under the needles, water and gas sit under tension. The roots are a wide shallow mat that reads vibration. Rain, a falling leaf, a mouse, a careful footstep mean nothing. Something Small or larger that bolts, jumps, or falls means the needles fire. After that the plant spends a minute swelling its air sacs back. Once in its life, after about twenty years underground, it puts up one enormous pale flower — cream-white with a faint green center — then dies. Scavengers walk the mats with exaggerated slowness and feed on what the needles drop. The plant does not eat the body. The soil does.

**The river cuts**

Wherever freshwater from the highland lake keeps the hundred-foot trees from closing, the canopy tears open. Light hits soil the closed jungle never feeds, and grass answers: blades eight feet tall, gold-green, thick enough to hide a standing person. Leave the water and the field is a second jungle that happens to be gold. A body moving through leaves a crushed corridor that lasts for hours.

Near the river the grass leans. Further in, some of it does not — that is an animal, not a plant.

**Razer-grass** grows in discrete waist-high islands of winter-white, not as a blanket. Each blade is living pale tissue inside a shell of cloudy opaline silica, thickest on the edges. The cutting edge is almost clear. The shell’s fractures scatter noon light so the stand reads as first frost on an island that has never seen frost. Wind moves the grass. The glitter does not. Walk it and it cuts. Break it and the shells shatter like a pane dropped on stone, then a glittering dust hangs in the air. Water soaks the dust down but makes the white harder to see. Fire burns the living tissue and leaves thousands of empty glass knives snapping under their own weight. Nothing large walks through it. Every trail bends around. Stands favor ground animals have already torn up — crossings, wallows, river bends, flood-scoured hollows. Damage makes the next growth thicker.

**Quickeye berries** grow in these valleys, rare. Small, glossy, almost black, bitter, leaving a wide-eyed shake behind the eyes. The cluster looks half-alive, like a handful of pupils. An animal that eats one bolts at a speed nothing should manage.

Along the water itself the current is green-blue over pale limestone. Long ribbons of submerged grass stream downstream. Fallen trees lie on the bottom. The river is tended — shade trees cut or dragged off the banks — so the water stays too clear.

**Breathmelon** is abundant on the wet margins. Pale green, light enough to float, rind dimpled like a lung. Thump it and it rings hollow. Cut it and the flesh is a lattice of air-cells that squeak. Underwater it leaks trapped gas. Eat one and you breathe in the current and swim as you walk. It sits exactly where the water is the best road.

**The sick interior**

Deeper in, every leaf is black at the edge. Bark splits and does not heal clean. New tissue comes in already failing. Fungi bloom in colors that belong to nothing else on the island. Exposed roots run above ground like veins, wrist-thick to thigh-thick, bright with sap, all pointing the same way. Cross one and the light pulses upslope.

**Whisper fig** grows on that Quiet-to-Rot edge. A small green fig, nothing until you split it: pale veins in the flesh like a nerve, and the air around the cut goes briefly quiet. The plant’s own signalling chemistry talks to animal minds for a few minutes after you eat it.

**Skybladder** hangs in the sealed Rot canopy. A pale bladder the size of a helmet, so light it tugs at the vine in still air. Skin thin as a drum. Flesh dry, fibrous, buoyant enough to lift a hand. Eat it and you take the air for an hour.

**Rotheart** grows on the sickest trees, very rare, and only once the spore-air has already started to wear a body down. Black-hearted, pulp the color of old blood, warm, faintly pulsing. Eating it feels like the plant’s last refusal to die, passed along: exhaustion, poison, and sickness leave, and rest after that goes further than it should.

**Corpsewood** is not living growth. It is a dead tree the root network still walks — forty feet, bark blackened and split, branches bare, roots dragged like a skirt of cables. It never goes faster than a walk. Fire is the one thing that stops it knitting back together. It stays in the tree line and patrols the deep interior.

Snakewood still lives here, larger and less healthy. Strangler figs still pinch old trails. The same hundred-foot canopy, sicker.

Under the roots, lava tubes take pale curtains of living root through the ceiling. Water ticks off the tips. That is surface forest reaching down, not a separate flora.

**The last clearing**

The luminous roots stop at a verge. No root crosses the two unmarked graves. The floor is grass too green for the air around it. Black flowers ring that grass and stay open. They are the mourner line. Visitors may step through. The animals at the tree line do not.

The **Death Bloom tree** is otherwise an ordinary fruit tree. Its kind is the fruit the buried companion loved; the island offered it at the burial and it has hung at perfect ripeness ever since — no blossom cycle, no green fruit, no fall. Take a piece off the branch and it begins aging like any other fruit. The tree, the two graves, the black-flower ring, and the bound soil are one living memorial. Destroy it and the first signs are small: wind crosses the clearing, a black flower closes, fallen fruit finishes rotting, an insect lands on it, leaves remember seasons.

**What the island is doing**

Nothing here is a new invention. Grass already puts silica in its blades. Figs already strangle. Lianas already use other trees as bone. Wetland plants already grow air-tissue. Pears already grit with stone cells. Plums already frost themselves with wax. Berries already pack pigment and sugar. A dying plant already throws chemistry at the world. Aruhe took the brakes off and left the design. The fruit works. The garden minds who takes it. The plants are the country.
