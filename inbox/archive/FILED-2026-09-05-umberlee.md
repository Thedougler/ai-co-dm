## Source verbatim

Umberlee is the sea as it actually is, not the sea as sailors wish it were. People in the Shattered Sea call her the Bitch Queen when they are angry and Queen of the Depths when they are trying not to be, but the name they use at the dock is just hers. She owns waves, storms, wrecks, and the kind of fear that makes a captain pay before he even checks the weather. She is not a mother of sailors and she is not a devil with a ledger. She is the water that will take a crown frigate and a smuggler’s skiff with the same hand, and the only thing she has ever asked in return is that you admit she can.

That admission is tribute. Every working harbour in the Scatter pays it. Crown, Concordat, independent free port, hidden Passage run — flags change, governors change, languages change. The shrine stays where a sailor can find it on the way in or the way out. A blessing from her people is only a receipt. It means you showed respect today. It has never meant you will come home.

The folk who collect that respect are the Waveservants. They wear salt-bleached grey. Their shrines smell of tallow and seawater. There is a basin at the door, candles that should have gone out in the wind, and a person with a booklet who would rather write your name than preach at you. Coin is easiest. Rope, fish bones, buttons, a compass needle, a carved token, or a spoken promise will do if that is what you have. Same rate for merchant, pirate, navy, smuggler, and pilgrim. A shrine that starts taking sides stops collecting from half the harbour, so they do not take sides. Neutrality is not a creed with them. It is how the work stays possible.

You can see the work in the architecture. In Kalowe the shrine is a stone niche in the causeway wall at the reef-gap, with a salvaged timber roof and one attendant who knows ships by what they owe. Every hull through that gap passes within reach of the basin. In Calveno it is a pale stone arch on the Mercatura canal, shallow enough that boats going past can read the tribute terms posted toward the water, not toward the door. In Port Tidefall it is cut into the cliff at the south end of the Dockfront, roof pressed low, tallow and salt first, a junior watching the candles while a senior handles the rest. These are not temples in the land sense. They are harbour furniture, as ordinary and as non-negotiable as a mooring post.

There is another kind of shrine, older and quieter, that does not sit on a working quay. On Vel-Orn in the Sunken Crown, where five small islands ring the Blue Hole, her house is cut into black stone on a cliff you can only land at if the surge is timed and you already know the way. Kelp wraps the pillars. Candles burn where wind should have killed them. Footsteps go soft. Inside they keep a ledger of drowned names and water-debt — tokens, coins, fish bones, carved shells, bits of ship and sailor kept as witness. Every object is a death written down. This is the older worship, aimed at the drowned rather than the living, and it is the place the Pearl of Souls used to rest over a tidal pool built for it. People at the table have sometimes called the site Vafnar, which is just a mishearing that stuck.

The Pearl is a fist-sized black thing with a sheen that will not sit still. Anyone who can feel death feels it before they see it. It is one of seven instruments of hers; the other six have not been found. It does one job without being asked: it takes the souls of people who drown near it and it keeps them. For years the Waveservants guarded it. Then Delmar Fisk, under a Chain Council commission that the Tessarine Concordat had arranged so Aldric Drave would not get there first, stole it from that Vel-Orn shrine. She answered in under a minute. Fog closed so thick the sea itself went invisible, and a trench opened under all five of his ships at once. They dropped eighty feet. The walls closed. The *Red Lady*, the *Narrow*, the *Heft*, the *Fernen*, and the *Loud Argument* went down together on the eastern Shelfworks, inside the Drowned Maw, below the line where her water stops being hers.

That line matters. The Maw is a puncture, not a metaphor — a trench-floor breach out of ordinary sea into the Elemental Plane of Water. Her claim ends where the water stops tasting like her water. She can sink a fleet on this side of it. She cannot reach in and take the Pearl back. So she did the thing she could do. She pressed the five captains into Delmar, the only man who walked away, and she left their crews in the Pearl. The Pearl is still working down there. It holds those crews and the people the Leviathan has killed since. It also broadcasts across the boundary. Things on the other side have started answering — the Leviathan, and smaller horrors that can still fit a narrow crack — and something larger is pushing. Recovering the Pearl would stop the signal. Leaving it calls the next thing to the next fissure.

That is why she has not simply killed Delmar. The captains ride him. If she takes him now, those five go into the Maw with him and she loses the only mortal who can still fetch what she cannot touch. Frustration is not the same as helplessness, and she has not been quiet about it.

In Calveno she used Branca. Branca is a senior Waveservant in her mid-forties, salt-white hair pulled back hard, a slight limp she stopped noticing years ago, and a little leather tide-table booklet she consults before she looks at you. Her warmth is the warmth of someone who has delivered bad news often enough to make it easy. Delmar ducked her once with a lie she believed, which only bought him a cycle. When he finally came to the harbour shrine she was the body the goddess borrowed. Umberlee spoke through a priestess’s mouth that was not using the priestess’s voice, snapped Branca’s neck in front of witnesses to prove the point, and put the breath back before the candles had time to drip. The price named that day was simple. Bring the Pearl. Then they would talk about the commissioners. Not before. Branca came back calling it a clarifying experience and meaning it. Her faith is not despite the cruelty. The cruelty is the faith.

Since Delmar sailed the Midchain instead of east toward the Maw, collection has gone personal. Weather arrives off the charts. Boats vanish in the party’s wake — holds dumped, crews gone, taken whole — and nobody on the docks has tied that talk to him yet. The hull doing the taking is the *Dead Lady*, which is the *Red Lady* raised and sent to work. She does not close to kill him. She holds station, knows his heading, and makes defiance cost more than compliance. Waveservants who would have treated him like any other captain now know him before he speaks. The Chain Council, who paid for the theft and would like the whole matter kept quiet, have been raising Kalowe’s shrine tribute to buy silence, which only teaches her clergy who else belongs on the ledger.

There is a second, older hoard that is not the Pearl. Sailors have always said she keeps the finest things the storms take. On the Sunken Crown seafloor, in tidal caves you can only reach at the decade’s lowest tides, those pieces lie scattered and unblemished — jewels that do not cloud, gold that does not tarnish, statues that look as if they were set down yesterday. That hoard is the Tithe of the Bitch Queen. Taking any piece puts her attention on the object itself. Squalls follow the ship that carries it until the piece touches saltwater again. Handing it to someone else just hands them the weather. Her own clergy will not say whether the Pearl counts as Tithe. They refuse to rule.

What people believe about her, when they are being honest, is three sentences. Pay. The sea does not negotiate. A blessing is not a promise. The tide does not care which flag you fly. Holy days and set ceremonies are thin on the ground because the work is daily and procedural. Offerings are whatever the harbour’s ledger asks. The mark you see carved into harbour stones and ship prows is a wave curling left to right. If she has a preferred tool in a mortal hand it is a trident or a harpoon, which is just the tool you use when the water has already decided.

She is not the only name said on a ship. Valkur lives in a muttered line at the rail — sailor courage, a hoped-for wind, private hope with no temple and no booklet. You do not pay him at the basin. You still pay her. Tyr is the land-and-paper counterweight, the god of oaths, commissions, and courts martial that the Dravosi Crown leans on when a warrant needs a name behind it. He does not own the wave. Auralis, later, is a constructed thing the Grung gold caste will try to pass as divine; that is a different kind of authority problem and not her problem. The Sentinels of the Eyrie built their whole monastic habit as a counter to Waveservant collection, which tells you how complete her hold on the harbours already was.

Underneath the daily tribute sits a suspicion her own clergy do not advertise. The payments may be what keeps the Maw’s fissure from opening the rest of the way. Stone with no known quarry comes up. Currents reverse against the tide tables. Ships go missing and the old records stay unread. If the ledgers go red at a major harbour and stay red, the sea itself starts answering the lapse. Challenge her hard enough and you are not only picking a fight with a goddess. You are picking a fight with the plug in the trench.

That is the shape of her in this water. She is worshipped the way you check the lines before you leave the quay — not with joy, not with despair, with the knowledge that the alternative is to pretend the sea is a road. She will take Crown and Concordat alike. She will use a smiling woman with a booklet. She will raise a dead flagship and send it to follow a man she cannot afford to kill. She wants her Pearl back because it is hers and because while it sits below her reach it is calling things she does not own. Delmar still owes it. Branca is still keeping the appointment. The basin at every working harbour is still lit. And out past the last charted sounding, the water that is not hers keeps pressing on the crack.
