## Source (verbatim Nick paste)

Hinewai was an elf from a small island village that kept to itself. Water sat in her name the way it sits in a well — Hinewai is a Māori woman’s name tied to water — and she grew into the kind of druid who did not just tend a grove, she was the person a place trusted. People like that do not think of land as property. They think of it as kin. She was immensely strong in that work, the sort of archdruid whose word could make a hillside listen.

Karath took that from her.

Karath is the loud island across a half-mile of open channel from Aruhe: tannin rivers the color of week-old tea, rainforest to the tide, Grung clans who do not hail ships. Their interior is hatchery country. Captive spellcasters get dosed and kept slow, then given just enough antidote to touch the Weave so they can push ordinary vermin into giant toads, snakes, and spiders. Hinewai was captured there and put on that work. Almost all of her autobiographical memory has since rotted away. What stuck is the hatred. She still hates the Grung. She still hates anyone who takes.

She fled with one companion. That is the person the whole island is built around, and we never got a name for him. They made the swim for the mute green country across the channel — close enough to see freedom, close enough to drown in sight of it. He went under in that water. She did not leave him there. She carried the body inland, days into a place that was already a living country, and buried him under a fruit tree. The island offered her the fruit he had loved. She took that as a vow.

Then she made the second grave her own.

The ritual is the old druid bargain taken past its breaking point: fuse soul and body to the land you mean to protect. It kills you. What remains is not a person with a box you can smash. The graves, the tree, the ring of black flowers, the bound soil, and the relationship between them are her. Smash an ordinary lich’s vessel and they stay gone. Cut Hinewai down and she comes back at that tree in a handful of days, so long as the memorial still stands. The grove is her body in the way a village is a family’s body. Roots run out from that shelf above the Clear Lake, wrist-thick to thigh-thick in the Rot, glowing with sap, all of them pointing home.

What she meant to do was keep him.

She kept roots off his bones. She kept animals off his grave. She kept settlers off the sand. She kept the fruit on his tree at the ripeness it had the day she laid him down. She kept change itself away from the one patch of ground that still meant him. That is not a failed preservation. That is a successful one. The horror is what success costs when the thing you are protecting is already dead.

Love, in her, did not cool. It metastasized.

She loved life so hard she would not let life finish. Growth lost its brakes. Reproduction lost restraint. Healing would not conclude. Adaptation would not stop. Rot would not complete. Every familiar thing on Aruhe still looks like itself and is doing the wrong job. Rabbits hunt in packs. A deer stands upright over a carcass and comes back after dark wearing a voice stolen from your fire. Grass armored itself with glass. Caterpillars explode into more of themselves. Dead trees get up and walk the Rot because even dead wood is not allowed to be finished. Fruit on the old terraces heals like a potion, hides you, puts strength in the arm, lets you breathe water — genuinely excellent food, no curse on the flesh itself. The garden simply minds who takes it. Take, and the nearest animals turn. Receive what the island offers, and that is a different sentence. She cannot stand waste, ownership, or careless destruction. Those who love life are still, in her remaining mind, friends. Those who harm life feed life instead.

The Grung named the place Hungry Isle from one survivor’s telling a generation back and will not land. Their boats work the western reefs. They treat any taking of fruit, fish, forage, or trapped game as a death sentence, because the last landing party that tried was hunted down by animals that turned on Grung scent.

Most of Hinewai is gone. What is left is a short list she can still hold: hatred of the Grung, hatred of invaders and takers, love of living things, love of Aruhe, and love for the man she carried uphill. She may not remember her own name. Concrete verbs still fit her mouth — take, break, cut, kill, carry, eat, drink, leave, return, grow, feed. Accusation lands when someone treats the garden like property: you do not protect this place; you own it. That is the knot she cannot untie.

Until you reach the Grove she is not a figure on the path. She is the silence. Birds stop. The Quiet is a country where sound dies. Fauna stay independent animals; she does not speak through a hawk or ride a squid like a puppet. A Crown Squid hunts like a Crown Squid. A Bloodhawk owns the open cuts because it is that kind of bird. Her presence is the island agreeing with itself.

The Grove is the one room where the appetite stops. Everywhere else, nature was prevented from stopping. Here, nature is prevented from continuing. A small clearing, a hundred and fifty feet across, too green for the sick air around it. One fruit tree over two unmarked graves. Black flowers in a ring. No root crosses either grave, while the rest of the island is hyper-fertile. Fruit hangs at the ripeness it had the hour he died. Flowers stay open. Predators gather at the tree line and watch. They do not hunt, graze, or cross. Terror-birds, walking deadwood, the squid, the upright deer — all of them become mourners. The air is sweet with rot that will not finish. Look. Remember. Do not touch. Nothing leaves. Nothing changes.

The first grave is his. The second is hers. They were dug years apart. Nobody stayed after the second one to tend it, because she went under. His grave is the reason. Hers is the mechanism. She refused to let the ordinary work of soil — roots, fungi, insects, seedlings — recycle him. That refusal became the law of an island the size of a country.

If you arrive without taking, she can meet you calm. Proud. Old. Courteous to people who stop to admire the place, who ask about the animals, who come as guests. That warmth drops cleanly the moment someone harms the garden. She states what will happen the way weather states what will happen. She does not threaten. Strike the tree and she weakens with it; her walking body is not the thing that matters. Ruin the Bloom and she stays dead. The Grove does not explode. Wind finally crosses the grass. A black flower closes. Fallen fruit finishes rotting. An insect lands on it. Over weeks the roots stop talking to each other. Over months the territories argue again. Over years the Quiet learns sound, fruit overripens and drops, some of her children vanish, and the island can be a place people might live on instead of a shrine that kills to keep one room still.

Leave the Bloom and the memorial endures. Come back in ten years and the fruit is the same, the flowers are open, the graves are clean, the mourners are waiting.

The question the island asks is not whether you can kill a lich. It is whether grief is allowed to own a living country forever. She loved a man, then she loved the ground he occupied, then she loved life itself so possessively that life became a tumor with his name on it. Protection turned into control. Abundance turned into excess. Survival turned into undeath. And the saddest part is the part that still works: if you stand in that clearing and do not touch anything, you can still hear, under the courtesy and the fury, a woman who only wanted the world to stop at the moment before she lost him.
