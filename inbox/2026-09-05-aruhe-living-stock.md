# Aruhe living stock (source)

Nick paste 2026-09-05. Ingest + Homebrewer: update/fill typed monster notes zone by zone. Canon frame: Aruhe does not invent new animals — it takes known ones and removes the brakes. The Blight gardens that pressure; she does not ride these bodies. They hunt like themselves.

## Frame

Aruhe does not invent new animals. It takes ones you already know and takes the brakes off. Size, hunger, family feeling, freeze-and-watch, camouflage, play — whatever the animal already did well, the island lets it keep doing that until the job is wrong. The gardener behind that pressure is the thing people call the Blight. She does not ride these bodies. They hunt like themselves.

What follows is the living stock, zone by zone.

## The beach and the reef gap

**Reef skulls** are hermit crabs that outgrew every natural shell on the reef. They wear what the wreckage gives them: a human skull, a plate of hull, a chunk of coral. Dog-sized, walking sideways through ankle-deep water. At low tide three to five bury in the shingle until only the top of the shell shows, then they erupt, clamp, and drag toward the undertow. They do not plan. They just all move at once, the way a swarm of ants does. A dinghy pulled onto this shingle is shade they already live under.

**Small blood hawks** work the tide-meat on the charted beach. Ordinary stooping hawks. They mark a wound. They do not lift a person. The huge bird that owns the sky inland is the same animal with the size cap gone.

## The old gardens and the first terraces

**Wolfrabbits** look like rabbits until they move. Wolf-sized, dark-striped, long ears and a twitching nose on a hunting-cat body. Packs of four to six work the collapsed terraces at dawn and dusk. They launch from a wall and cover thirty feet in one bound. Warrens honeycomb the terrace stone. When one goes down the others eat it — not from spite, from hunger the island cannot feed. They will not den past the Old Mouth once daylight dies in the tube. Something down there outranks them. They know the white razer-grass stands and jump them rather than land in them. They have learned the strike-lanes of snakewood and will drive prey under those branches on purpose. They will break off a pounce for the smell of a grubnade.

**Grinning apes** sit in the terrace canopy in threes and fives. Gorilla-sized, dark-furred, faces locked in a rictus that never changes — lips back, eyes wide. They do not groom. They do not call. The grin is muscle that cannot go anywhere else. Stones first, then they drop as a group on whatever moved below.

**Vine lashes** hang over trails like every other rope in the canopy: shoulder-thick, no leaves, no fruit. They wait. Something walks under, they whip, wrap, and squeeze. They drink through the roots. This is the first lesson the terraces teach — some vines grab.

**Grubnades** are thumb-long cream-green caterpillars working the throats of flowers. Touch one, shake the plant, burn it, and two dark lines rush together under the skin. It swells, hisses like fat in a pan, and pops. The flash is small and hot. What crawls away afterward are her daughters, blown ten to thirty feet into the leaves. There is no moth. The explosion is how the species breeds. Most things that can learn have learned not to bite the fat pale ones. Feeding marks stay on the leaves because nothing eats the eater. Otters, deer-stalkers, and wolfrabbits all give them room.

**Unsaid macaws** live in the orchards, the grassland edges, the lake margin. Healthy birds. Too bright, clear-eyed. They eat fruit and nuts and the odd shellfish. What they do with voices is the job pushed too far. A macaw hears the thought you almost said and speaks it in your voice. Flocks teach each other phrases, so later you cannot tell if the bird is reading you or repeating last week’s flock-talk. They do not belong in the Quiet. That country is supposed to have no birdsong.

Young **snakewood** starts here. The sixty-foot adults live farther in so they do not replace the vine lashes.

## The quiet and the rot floor

This is closed country. Sight dies in a few paces. The floor is not where the largest hunter lives.

**Crown squid** live in the crowns. Sixty to eighty feet of rubbery mantle and eight working arms, plus a forest of finer lines that can drop another eighty to a hundred feet. The mantle is a gas bag. Living, the animal barely loads the trees. Dead, it weighs tons. It does not swing. It flows, like a jumping spider the size of a house. Skin matches bark until a principal eye rolls inside the clear head to look at you. That roll is the tell. It watches a trail for minutes, picks the isolated one, hooks an ankle, and lifts. It likes a chase. Open grassland and occupied river water are the two places it will not go on purpose. Juveniles sometimes work the upper terraces. The oldest sit heavier in the Rot, where food is easy. A shredded sixty-foot vine hanging from a branch is their leftover, not a snakewood kill.

**Snakewood** is a clonal liana using the canopy as a skeleton. At rest it is a bundle of ordinary vines. When it strikes, five or six stems braid into a wedge, clap shut, and the body on the ground goes up. No head, no eyes, no hiss. Tiny sweet flowers line the inner face of the “jaws,” then vanish when they close. Pale sap does the digesting in the air, like a starfish working outside its body. Cut one strand and the neighbors take the load. Trails that swing wide around an ordinary-looking stretch are deer-stalkers refusing to walk under it. What you find on the floor are antlers, a boot, feathers — everything edible went up.

**Deer-stalkers** were deer. Hips turned wrong, forelimbs ended in knife-claws, eyes set forward. Seven to nine feet, always looking starved. They stand on a carcass like a sick animal, crash away too loud when disturbed, and come back from the trees minutes later locked on whoever was most alone or who last touched the meal. They hunt the dim edge. They play back voices they have already heard — a name from last night’s fire, held too wet and too long. They will not walk razer-grass. They will not contest a bear-elk or a terror-bird. Two in the same stretch ignore each other.

**Thornbacks** are boar and porcupine with the off-switch gone. Javelin quills on the shoulders and rump, tusks grown through the skull and out again. Solitary. Two in one stretch fight each other first. They root the Quiet floor and shove trails other animals use afterward. Spent spines lie everywhere and grow back in hours.

**Bear-elk** work the boundary between Quiet and Rot. Elk rack on a bear’s chest, bigger than a draft horse, low and wide. Antlers drip a faint luminous sap that is not the animal’s own. Pale eyes, no pupil. It runs a fixed daily beat along scored trees. It does not need to eat often. Terror-birds step aside for it. The crown squid is the one thing that takes it from above. The bloodhawk takes it when it steps into a cut of open sky.

**Strangler figs** grow singly on the game trails that already squeeze through stone. Aerial roots hang in curtains. The roots reach. The trunk is hollow. Bones show in the bark where old kills grew into the wood. The tree does not walk. It does not need to.

**Corpsewood** is a dead tree walking. Forty feet, bark split, roots dragging. It patrols the deep Rot on a seasonal beat and does not leave the tree line. Fire is the one thing that stops it knitting itself back together. The wood died years ago. What moves it did not.

**Silence moths** drift the Rot and the Grove approaches at dusk and dawn in a cloud the size of a sail. Individually they are thumb-sized and colorless. Ahead of the swarm the jungle goes quiet, then the cloud fills a mouth. Fire drives them off. They will not follow you out of the trees.

## Grass, river, and the clear lake

**Terror-birds** own the open cuts and the shaded rims. Taller than a horse, black, moss growing out of the feathers so a still bird reads as a trunk with a yellow eye. Serrated beak, teeth when it gapes, talons like forearms. They do not fly. Each holds about a quarter-mile and will not share it. They stand. The ground shakes before you see the rush. One charge is the commitment. Prey that reaches eight-foot grass, water, or a white razer stand ends the hunt. A fed bird is a mossed shape and a tremor that does not close. Flattened digest-circles hold bone and a sour bolus. Sailors who have only heard stories call this a roc that lost the sky. It is not. That bird is still flying.

**Bloodhawks** — the big ones — own the hole in the canopy. Charcoal body, crimson-on-black flight feathers, shaggy red nape, cream hook-beak, amber eye, talons the size of a rowboat, eighty to a hundred feet of wing. They circle a valley or the lake bowl on a thermal and do not scream. The tell over land is a moving bar of dark on the floor. Over water it is the crimson underwing. They take whales, canoes in open channel, bear-elk that step into a cut, a terror-bird that holds a rim too long in hard noon. They will not stoop closed canopy or eight-foot grass — the wings tear. They do not work the charted shingle. They do not put a foot in otter water. A crushed grass circle with no exit trail, or bear-elk bones on terrace stone with no approach tracks, is their work. Skybladder fruit hangs on Rot-edge vines that already lean into light; the bird does not eat it. The fruit is for whatever needs to reach cover before the shadow-bar arrives.

**River otters** own the Long Reach and the lake. Twelve feet nose to tail, chocolate-black, pale throat marks unique as fingerprints. Families of four to six adults and a few pups. The lake family is larger; the matriarch there is older than the wreck on the beach. They keep the water gin-clear on purpose — they cull the grazers, drop the shade-trees, patrol the banks. They will play with a rope, an oar, an ankle, a deer-stalker’s antlered head tossed in the shallows after the meal is finished. Blood kin only. Anything that enters the water is in the game. Harm one, or touch a pup, and play stops. They vanish. Then one person is hit from below and the rest see only wakes. Five minutes later the pups wrestle again. They will not haul a kill onto razer-grass. They sniff a grubnade and leave it. A crown squid that trails a line into occupied water meets six of them pulling the other way.

Silver schools of thumb-length fish feed that garden. Capybara-shaped grazers and waterfowl feed it too. Those are groceries, not names.

## Under the island

**Cave bats** have three- to four-foot wings. They leave at dusk through skylights and feed over the canopy. Their guano is what the cave eats. A colony taking flight all at once means something farther in already moved.

**Root roaches** cluster pale and hand-sized around the wells where surface roots punch the ceiling. Harmless. They vanish into cracks seconds before the rail arrives.

**Glass crickets** live in those same wells — long-legged, long-antennaed, pale. Their jumping is sometimes the only motion at a well. When a heavy vibration enters the basalt they go still.

**Blackrails** are the train in the tube. Thirty to forty-five feet of cave centipede. The back looks like parallel strips of wet black iron. Every wall, ceiling, and floor is a road. Antennae longer than a man read air. The body reads the rock. Eyesight is poor. It listens first — dust jumps in rings — then the leg-rattle comes around the bend, then the head comes along the wall sideways. It pins with body-segments and the forcipules do the rest. Venom meant for deer. It carries the quiet ones away. One adult owns miles of main tube. Juveniles a foot or three long mean the adult is not here; adults eat unrelated young. The Great Bore is the best road in the cave, which is why the rail uses it.

## How they sit together

The island is a set of claims, not one king.

- Closed canopy belongs to the squid.
- The hole in the canopy belongs to the bloodhawk.
- Eight-foot grass and the valley floor belong to the terror-bird.
- Occupied river belongs to the family.
- Main tubes belong to the rail.
- The beach shallows belong to the crabs and the small hawks.
- The first terraces belong to packs, grinning faces, and grabbing vines.
- The mute floor belongs to still-hunters, thorned rooters, and plants that wait over a trail.

Run from one and you are usually standing in the next one’s country. That is the whole arrangement.
