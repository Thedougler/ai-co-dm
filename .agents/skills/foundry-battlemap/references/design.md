# Tactical brief

Write this brief before touching the prompt template. Every field feeds a slot in the Build step. The brief is not sent to Imagine — it is the design the prompt communicates.

## Zone plan

A zone is a spatial region with a tactical job. Name at least two. State each zone's position, rough footprint, and what it does for the encounter.

The zone plan is the macro read — what a player sees at thumbnail before studying individual squares. Zones separate by value and silhouette: a stone dais reads differently from a grassy approach lane, and both differ from a water boundary.

Example (ceremonial hall):
> Three zones on the long axis: open approach lane (south half, cream marble, wide), circular arena (center, green-gold medallion floor, 15-square diameter), raised sanctum (north, throne dais with gold canopy, elevated 5 ft).

Example (jungle camp):
> Four zones in a radial layout: central bonfire clearing (open staging, warm earth, 10-square radius), perimeter stall ring (tents and vendors, cover-dense), rocky cliff border (impassable edge, foliage), river margin (south, shallow water, difficult terrain).

## Route grammar

Name the primary route (the obvious toe-to-toe movement lane) and at least one alternate route (flanking, bypass, or hidden). State chokepoints and threshold crossings where routes narrow or force a decision.

A map with only one path between two important areas creates a queue, not a fight. Two or more connected routes create tactical choice.

Example:
> Primary: red carpet axis from south entrance to throne. Alternate: lateral edge lanes on both sides of the carpet, behind statue cover. Chokepoint: the gap between equestrian statues at the arena ring.

## Cover and blocker inventory

Name at least three items that block line of sight, provide cover, or create elevation. State each item's grid-scale footprint (e.g., "2×2 squares") and tactical effect (half cover, full LoS block, elevation, difficult terrain).

Cover and blockers turn open floor into a decision space. Place them where routes cross or where staging areas need protection. Concentrate decorative detail at edges and around landmarks — the movement lanes stay open.

Example:
> 1. Equestrian statues — 2×2 squares, full LoS blocker, four placed at cardinal points of the arena ring.
> 2. Side recesses — 2×3 alcoves flanking the carpet, three-quarter cover, two per side.
> 3. Throne dais — 4×3 raised platform (5 ft elevation), half cover from lower floor.

## Staging reservation

Name the area(s) where open floor is reserved for tokens. Staging is the negative space that makes the map playable — detail and props do not seal it.

State the staging area's position and approximate size in squares. A standard medium encounter needs at least one open area of 5×5 squares or larger.

Example:
> Open approach lane (south half, ~15×20 squares clear). Arena ring interior (10-square diameter clear around the medallion).

## Elevation reads

List elevation changes that stay readable from overhead. Stairs, dais, pit, balcony, bank, and bridge communicate height through shadow, material change, and contour — not labels.

Skip elevation that would be invisible or ambiguous from a straight-down camera. A second storey that requires a separate layer belongs in multi-level mode.

## Material ladder

Rank three or more materials from dominant (most area, neutral value, walkable ground) to accent (least area, saturated, focal landmark). The dominant material keeps the play surface readable. Saturated color and strong contrast reserve attention for routes, zones, and landmarks.

Example:
> 1. Cream marble floor (dominant — walkable ground, neutral).
> 2. Red carpet (route marker — saturated, guides the eye).
> 3. Green-gold geometric tile (zone marker — arena ring, high contrast).
> 4. Gold gilding (accent — throne, statues, ornamentation, highest value).

## Authored identity

One sentence naming the culture, function, or story this map's architecture and props serve. Every prop should feel placed with intent for that identity. A map where props vary and tell one coherent story reads as a specific place; a map with generic repeated stamps reads as a tile set.

Example:
> Ottoman-baroque royal audience hall built for ceremony, intimidation, and processional theatre.

## FRAME

Choose the aspect ratio from the tactical footprint — how the zones and routes lay out in space. Default is portrait 9:16. Override when the site demands it.

| Label | Ratio | Squares (W × H) | When |
|---|---|---|---|
| portrait | 9:16 | 25×45 | most encounters, vertical sites |
| long-axial | ~9:24+ | 17×48 or fitted | long halls, ship decks, canyons, processional spaces |
| square | 1:1 | 30×30 | arenas, tower floors, circular clearings |
| wide | 16:9 | 45×25 | wide decks, bridges, shoreline stretches |

Pick the ratio that fits the zone plan without dead space. State the chosen ratio and square dimensions in the brief.

## Completion

The brief is done when it names:
- At least two zones with position and tactical job
- A primary route and at least one alternate route
- Three or more cover/blockers with grid-scale footprint
- One staging area with approximate size
- A material ladder of three or more materials
- An authored identity sentence
- A FRAME ratio and square dimensions

Vehicle and multi-level modes add their own fields — see [modes.md](modes.md).
